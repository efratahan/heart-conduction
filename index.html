    Anatomy. Heart, valves, great vessels and coronary arteries from BodyParts3D, © The Database Center for Life Science, licensed under CC BY-SA 2.1 Japan. Meshes were decimated and recoloured. The model derived from it here (decimated meshes, reconstructed ventricular wall, recolouring, and the modelled conduction tissue placed on it) is likewise licensed CC BY-SA 2.1 Japan. BodyParts3D has the four cavities and both atrial walls but no ventricular wall: the ventricular muscle here is reconstructed around the real cavities (left ~1.1 cm, right ~0.45 cm, the septum is what lies between), and the real coronary arteries sit on its surface. Conduction-system positions are placed at their anatomical landmarks (SA node at the SVC junction, AV node at the triangle of Koch, His at the membranous septum, fascicles to the papillary muscles).
    What is real and what is modelled. Scanned anatomy (BodyParts3D): the atrial walls, the four cavities, valves, papillary muscles, aorta, pulmonary trunk, venae cavae, pulmonary veins, and the coronary arteries and veins, shown as they are. Modelled on anatomical landmarks (no open dataset has them as meshes; BodyParts3D and Z-Anatomy were checked): the ventricular wall around the real cavities, and the whole conduction system as 3D tissue: SA node as a crescent at the SVC junction, AV node as an oval in the triangle of Koch, His bundle through the membranous septum, right bundle to the moderator band, left bundle as a fan to the anterior and posterior fascicles, Purkinje fibres on the endocardium (simulated, not drawn, to keep the view clear), and three internodal pathways with Bachmann's bundle. Whether the internodal pathways are discrete tracts or preferential routes through atrial muscle is debated; the signal does travel along them. The wall shown is carved away under the arteries so they lie in their grooves; the simulated muscle continues beneath them, as in life.
    Where the leads look from. The chest electrodes sit at their places on this chest: V1 and V2 in the 4th intercostal space either side of the sternum, V4 to V6 one rib space lower in the 5th (mid-clavicular, anterior and mid-axillary), V3 between; each chest lead looks from the heart centre toward its electrode. The limb leads use the frontal plane: Einthoven's triangle between the right arm, left arm and left leg electrodes, and the six limb leads as the hexaxial wheel; a lead's positive end is where it looks from. The right-leg electrode is the ground.
    How the ECG is made. Each point of muscle gets a voltage over time once the wave reaches it. At every moment the voltage differences between neighbouring points add up to one arrow, the heart vector, and each lead is how much that arrow points toward it. Nothing is drawn by hand: cutting a bundle or blocking an artery changes the tissue, and the ECG follows.
    The membranous septum. The top of the septum, between the valves, is fibrous rather than muscle, so in the model it never fires and adds nothing to the ECG. Without it, normal V1 is rS.
    Ischaemia, as modelled. STEMI: the injured territory loses plateau voltage through the whole wall (ST raised over it). Subendocardial ischaemia: only the inner layer of the left ventricle loses plateau voltage, so the injury current points inward (ST depressed over the wall, raised in aVR). Ischaemic T inversion: in the LAD territory the action potential is prolonged and the recovery order through the wall is reversed. Each is a single simplified change to the tissue; the ECG follows from it.
    Simplified on purpose. One muscle speed, one action-potential shape, idealised lead directions. Coronary territories: septum front two-thirds LAD, back third RCA; elsewhere the artery nearest the overlying surface. In this heart the normal axis comes out at about −25° (normal, leaning left). Greyed items in the list are not in the model yet; they will not be faked.
